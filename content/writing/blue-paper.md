The paper is the pale blue of an airmail envelope, and so thin the sheet in front of you carries the shadow of the one beneath. Smythson calls it featherweight, has made it since the Edwardians, and guards the furnish of it the way distilleries guard mash bills. It takes fountain pen ink without complaint, which paper this light has no business doing.

Frank Smythson opened his stationery shop on New Bond Street in 1887, and the Panama notebook followed in 1908 — a travel diary bound soft in crossgrain lambskin, light enough for a coat pocket, named in the same spirit as the hat: equipment for going somewhere warm and coming back with notes. The gilding on the page edges is not decoration, or not only; gilt seals the paper against damp and dirty thumbs. Every part of the object is doing quiet double duty, which is the house style of things designed before design was a word anyone used.

The A5 cover is the format grown up. A lambskin jacket that takes a replaceable book of that blue paper, which changes the arithmetic of the thing entirely: the notebook is no longer the object. The cover is. Refills come and go — a year of meetings, a season of lists — and the leather stays, softening at the spine, rounding at the corners, taking on the exact curvature of wherever it rides. The contents rotate; the container compounds.

There is a wager embedded in that construction. A bound notebook assumes you will finish it and shelve it. A covered one assumes you will keep writing for longer than any single book can hold — that the habit will outlast the pages. Selling that assumption in lambskin, at Smythson's prices, is either optimism or flattery, and the customer decides which.

The shop in the photograph is dressed for Christmas, its windows built out as toy theatres, the pavement wet in the way Bond Street photographs best. Inside, on shelves, sit stacks of pale blue paper waiting to be someone's year.
