Look closely at the photograph and there it is: a scratch, dead across the polished clasp, on a case fitted with screws you can see and parts you can order. The scratch will stay. The clasp could be replaced in an afternoon by anyone with a screwdriver, and mostly they aren't, for reasons a spreadsheet cannot hold.

The Classic Flight is the Rimowa that still looks like the Rimowa of 1937 — the year Richard Morszeck built the company's first aluminium trunk in Cologne, out of the same material the aviation industry was learning to trust. The corrugated skin of the Junkers transports of that era solved a structural problem: thin metal, folded, becomes stiff. Rimowa borrowed the fold. Eighty-odd years later the fold is the brand, and the aeroplane that inspired it is in museums.

What separates the Classic (the line dropped "Flight" from its name after the change of ownership, though nobody who owns one noticed) from the rest of the modern catalogue is what it refuses. No polycarbonate. No integrated electronics. Leather handles that will darken with hands. An aluminium shell that records every belt loader from here to wherever. It is the least convenient case Rimowa makes and the one people photograph.

The construction is the argument. Riveted, not welded; screwed, not glued. Every hinge, latch, wheel and handle is a part, and parts imply a future — the case is designed on the assumption that it will live long enough to need one. That assumption used to be ordinary. It now reads as a position.

The TSA lock in the photograph sits at 0-0-9, one digit from factory. Somewhere there is an owner who set a combination, or meant to.
