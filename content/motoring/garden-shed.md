The door shuts with a clank that belongs to a toolbox, not a car. The steering wheel sits close and upright. The wipers are slow.

"I bought it because I wanted something appropriate for a fire trail," he says. "Turns out it is appropriate anywhere."

## Before this one

The Defender replaced a modern SUV. The previous car was comfortable and quiet, with a reversing camera and lane-keep assist. Its owner barely thought about it after stepping out.

The replacement is a white, short-wheelbase 90 with a 2.4-litre TDCi diesel and an automatic conversion. It has Boost alloys, BFG All-Terrains, a Safari Equippers rack and a Melville & Moon spare-tyre cover faded unevenly by the sun.

It lives near Katoomba, where the mornings are cold and the roads into town are narrow enough that oncoming traffic requires a nod and a folded mirror. It also spends time outside cafes in Leura and in Parramatta Road traffic.

## The marks

The paint tells the story better than the odometer. A rub along the left rear quarter from a sandstone gatepost that was closer than it looked. Stone chips across the bonnet from the unsealed stretch past Lithgow. A scratch on the passenger door that nobody can explain but everyone blames on the kids.

The side steps have been scuffed by boots of every size. The rear door hinge is starting to feel its age — it holds, but it lets you know. Mud has been hosed off so many times that the wheel arches have a permanent brown shadow that no amount of Gurney will shift.

A new Defender can look like a press car. This one belongs to someone.

## From the driver's seat

The driver sits high and upright, with the flat bonnet in view. Every corner of the car is easy to place on the road.

At idle there is diesel clatter. Under load, the turbo whistles. Above 80, the children shout from the back seat to be heard over the wind.

On the school run, a six-year-old leans from the rear window wearing a cap that reads COAL.

## Fifteen minutes of heat

The heater takes fifteen minutes to mean anything in a Katoomba winter. The auto conversion is competent but not intuitive — it hunts between second and third on the steep climb up the highway, and you learn to back off the throttle early to settle it down.

The turning circle requires planning. Three-point turns become five-point turns. Parking in Leura on a Saturday takes patience.

The shape does little for fuel economy. Its owner no longer checks the number at each fill.

## A book for the glovebox

Ben Fogle's *Land Rover* lives in the door pocket. It is a collection of stories about people who have built their lives around the vehicles.

## School, fire trail, servo

This one goes to school, the fire trail and the servo at six in the morning. It gets scratched and hosed down. The brown shadow in the wheel arches remains.
