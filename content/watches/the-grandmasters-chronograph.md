The one in the photograph has never been worn. White dial, tag on the bracelet, sitting in its presentation box like evidence. Someone bought one of the great steel sports chronographs and then — stopped. What was he saving it for? The question is better than any answer, so it stays.

The watch is a Royal Oak Chronograph, reference 25860ST, and the version this study is actually about is its blue-dialled brother — the one collectors took to calling the Kasparov. Garry Kasparov spent the late 1990s as an Audemars Piguet ambassador, then the highest-rated chess player who had ever lived, and the blue Royal Oak chronograph became attached to him the way watches do: worn in photographs, noticed by the kind of people who notice, nicknamed without anyone asking permission. The name outlasted the arrangement. Nicknames always do.

It took Audemars Piguet twenty-five years to put a chronograph in the Royal Oak at all. Gérald Genta drew the original in 1972 — the luxury sports watch in steel, priced like gold when steel was supposed to be cheap, the octagonal bezel with its eight visible screws — and the shape carried the company through decades when little else did. The chronograph arrived only in 1997, for the anniversary, running the calibre 2385. A quarter-century of restraint, then three subdials arranged so quietly you could miss them across a table.

That is the peculiar mercy of the 25860ST: it is a chronograph that doesn't perform being one. The tapisserie dial does its small geometry, the pushers sit flush with the case's own logic, and the whole thing reads as a Royal Oak first and a stopwatch somewhere around fourth. On the blue dial the counters ride in silver, and the effect in changing light is the reason the reference has a nickname and a following rather than merely a production run.

Kasparov retired from chess in 2005 and went on to harder games. The blue chronographs went on without him, gaining the scratches he presumably put on his own.

The white one in the photograph is still waiting to start.
