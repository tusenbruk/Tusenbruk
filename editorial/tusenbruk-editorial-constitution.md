# Tusenbruk — Editorial Constitution

*This document is not for publication. It is the standard every piece is held against before it goes up.*

---

## What Tusenbruk Is

Tusenbruk is a publication about the relationship between a person and the objects they use. Not collect. Use.

We cover watches, cameras, pens, notebooks, folios, attaches, cars, and luggage — because these are the objects that accompany a life being lived. They share grease and altitude and deadline and rain. They accumulate evidence. They become, eventually, irreplaceable — not because of what they're worth, but because of what they've been through.

We are not a buyer's guide. We are not a collector's resource. We are not a review site.

We are a publication about use. The story is the product.

---

## The Three Tests

Before any piece publishes, it must pass all three.

**Test one: Can you remove the person?**
If the human being at the centre of the piece could be extracted without losing anything essential, the piece isn't finished. Objects without people are catalogues. Tusenbruk publishes portraits. The object is how we see the person. The person is why we care about the object.

**Test two: Is there a detail only this person could have given you?**
Not a spec. Not a review-site observation. Something that happened, once, to this person, with this thing. The scratch from the door of a Land Cruiser. The folio that survived a customs search in Istanbul. The pen that ran out during the only contract that mattered. If the detail could belong to anyone, find a better one.

**Test three: Does the ending earn its silence?**
The best Tusenbruk pieces stop one beat before the obvious conclusion. The reader should feel the ending before they reach it. If the last line explains what the piece just showed, cut it. If the second-to-last line is stronger, the last line is wrong.

## What We Are Not Interested In

**Provenance without consequence.** We don't care when a reference was introduced, how many were made, or what it sold for at auction — unless that fact has changed what the person does with it. History matters when it lands in the present. Otherwise it's trivia.

**Condition.** Pristine is not a virtue here. A watch described as "kept in its original box" is a watch that has failed its owner.

**The acquisition story.** How someone got a thing is the least interesting version of the story. What they did with it after is the piece.

**Enthusiasm without specificity.** "I love this camera" is not a sentence. "The shutter release is stiff enough that I fire fewer frames, and most of the good ones were accidents" is a sentence.

**The product as hero.** Objects don't have feelings. They don't deserve reverence. They deserve use. Write about them accordingly.

---

## Voice

Tusenbruk writes in the tradition of writers who trusted the specific detail to carry the weight: David Coggins, Michael Hainey, Malcolm Gladwell at his most restrained, John McPhee at his most precise.

The characteristics of that tradition:

**The unobvious angle.** Every object has an obvious story. Don't write it. The obvious story about a Leica is the image quality. The unobvious story is the way its weight changes how you hold your arm for six hours in a market in Oaxaca. Find the thing nobody thought to look at. Start there.

**Specificity as argument.** A precise detail does more persuasive work than a general claim. "Expensive" tells the reader nothing. "The price of a second-hand Golf GTI" tells them everything. Name the actual thing. Give the exact weight. Use the real year.

**Economy without coldness.** Short sentences are not the goal. Sentences that carry exactly what they need to carry, and no more, are the goal. Some of those sentences will be long. Most will be shorter than the writer's first instinct.

**The knowing aside.** Coggins' signature move: the wry parenthetical that assumes the reader is in the room. Use it sparingly. Once per piece, if earned. Never as a substitute for having something to say.

**Restraint at the emotional peak.** When the piece arrives at its most human moment — the object that survived the thing, the detail that reveals the person — back off. Don't explain. Don't underline. State it plainly and move on. The reader will feel it without being told to.

---

## What Good Looks Like

A Tusenbruk piece makes someone feel seen in their relationship with their things. Not validated — seen. There is a difference. Validation says: you are right to love this. Recognition says: I noticed the same thing you noticed, and here is the evidence.

The reader should finish a piece and think: that was about a watch, but it was really about something else. Without being able to say exactly what.

That's the target. Everything else is in service of that.

---

## On Length

Long enough to earn the ending. Not a word longer.

Most pieces will run 800–1,400 words. Some will be shorter. None should be longer unless the person and the object genuinely require it. Length is not a signal of seriousness. It is a cost the reader pays. Charge them only what the piece is worth.

---

## On Photography

A Tusenbruk photograph shows the object in use, or after use. Never on a white background. Never arranged. The scratch should be visible. The desk should be real. If there is a hand in the frame, it should look like it works for a living.
