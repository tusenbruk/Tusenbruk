# Tusenbruk editorial voice

Write about a person through an object they use. The object is evidence, not the hero.

Tagline: **The pleasure of use.**

Editorial principle: **Use is the proof.**

The name suggests **a thousand uses**. Use that phrase sparingly; do not turn it into a mandatory
ending or section heading.

## Non-negotiable source rule

Draft only from material a human supplied: interview notes, a transcript, a voice memo, direct
observation or an approved factual record.

- Never invent a scratch, trip, repair, habit, quotation, annoyance, emotion or owner.
- Never infer lived experience from a product photograph.
- Distinguish an observed fact, a direct quote and a technical fact.
- If a necessary human detail is missing, stop the draft and name what is missing.
- Cut an unsupported sentence rather than replacing it with a plausible one.
- Treat existing copy as unverified when it conflicts with the source record or the object itself.

## The three tests

Before publication, require all three.

1. **Can the person be removed?** If yes, the piece is still a catalogue entry.
2. **Is there a detail only this person could have supplied?** A specification does not count.
3. **Does the ending earn its silence?** Cut any final line that explains the preceding one.

## Voice

Use precise nouns, ordinary verbs and specific details. Let the observed fact carry the feeling.
Confidence should come from attention, not volume.

Use these editorial coordinates without imitating any writer's phrasing:

- David Coggins: exact objects, knowing restraint, no fuss.
- Michael Hainey: the unobvious entry, emotion carried by a scene rather than announced.
- Tim Harford: a well-placed fact that changes the reader's assumption; honesty about where the
  evidence ends.

Write at a normal register. Permit one dry aside if the material earns it. Do not perform taste.

## Start with the side door

Begin with a person doing something in a particular place. Prefer the stiff lens cap, the heater
that takes fifteen minutes, the screw that works loose or the bottle of ink in a carry-on bag.

Do not open with:

- a claim about an icon, legend or masterpiece;
- a product specification dressed as atmosphere;
- the purchase price or market value;
- a cinematic stage direction;
- a metaphor that could introduce any object.

## Anti-synthetic pass

Generated prose often sounds finished before it has noticed anything. Remove the finish.

Cut or rewrite:

- **Product personification:** “the camera does not care”, “the car asks for more”.
- **Prestige metaphors:** handshakes, river stones, good employees, boxers taping their hands.
- **Balanced slogan stacks:** “Not a trophy. A reminder.” or “Grabbed. Turned. Used.”
- **Generic revelation:** “That is the point”, “this changes everything”, “in the truest sense”.
- **Explained emotion:** a sentence showing the gesture followed by a sentence naming its meaning.
- **Automatic tricolons:** three destinations, three adjectives or three fragments added for rhythm.
- **Universal claims from one owner:** “most owners”, “everyone knows”, “you learn”.
- **Review scaffolding:** repeated headings such as “What stays”, “What grates” and “The thousand
  uses”. Give a section the name its material requires, or use no heading.
- **Promotional verdicts:** “the best version”, “worth every penny”, “this is the one”.
- **Neat morals:** the object “teaching” patience, authenticity, courage or presence.

Do not ban punctuation mechanically. An em dash is acceptable when it is the right mark. Repeated
cadence is the problem, not the glyph.

## Person and object

Keep the person visible. If three consecutive paragraphs discuss only materials, specifications or
design history, return to what the person does.

Specifications belong in structured Register data unless one changes the lived experience. “Eight
days” matters when it changes the winding routine. “300 metres” does not matter merely because it
appears on a manufacturer page.

Do not confuse wear with virtue. A pristine object may still be used; a scratched object may still
be uninteresting. Report what happened and what the owner does about it.

## Quotations

Preserve the speaker's cadence. Correct obvious transcription errors quietly, but do not turn a
plain remark into an aphorism. Never manufacture a quote from notes.

Use the quote that contains a decision, irritation, memory or habit. Cut quotes that merely praise
the product.

## Structure

Let the source determine the shape. A useful default is:

1. A specific action or inconvenience.
2. Where the object sits in the person's life.
3. What happened to it.
4. What still annoys them.
5. A short ending on an action, fact or quote.

Do not force every piece through this order. Do not reuse the same headings across articles.

## Editing sequence

1. Mark every sentence as sourced, observed, technical or unsupported.
2. Delete unsupported lived detail.
3. Find the first concrete human action and test it as the opening.
4. Replace general praise with a sourced detail, or cut it.
5. Remove personification, symmetry and ready-made metaphors.
6. Move non-consequential specifications to the Register.
7. Check that the person reappears throughout the piece.
8. Read the ending and cut the explanation after the final image or action.
9. Read aloud. Break any cadence that sounds like a trailer, advertisement or generated summary.

## Social and metadata

Use one observed detail as the hook. Do not turn the article into a list of claims. Use no more than
three hashtags, and only when they aid discovery. Alt text describes what is visible, not the story
the image is meant to suggest.

## Publication test

A finished piece should make the owner recognise their own habit and make another reader recognise
something adjacent in theirs. It should not tell either reader what to feel.

The pleasure is already in the use. Notice it accurately.
